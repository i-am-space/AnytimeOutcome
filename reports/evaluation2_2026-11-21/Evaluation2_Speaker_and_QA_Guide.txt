Slide 3 · A safe prefix can be delivered
before queued tail work finishes
55 seconds · cumulative 2:30 · MECHANISM VALIDATED
Say this
Suppose four tokens are complete and a fifth draft step is already running. Submitted work
means the CPU has queued GPU commands, not that they have finished. The earlier uniform
path waits for that work before handoff. Our overlap path copies only the eligible completed
prefix into independent storage and can deliver it while the queued tail finishes. It still waits
before reusing the producer’s KV and graph buffers. That preserves memory ownership and
avoids stale reads. We observed response-before-tail events at K ten in engineering gates, so
the mechanism exists. That alone is not evidence of lower request latency.
Technical detail if asked
Normal K10 gate recorded 633 responses before tail completion across both protocols.
Shared-GPU/0.10-KV gate timings are diagnostic. High stream priority alone does not
guarantee concurrency.
Transition
To test whether this mechanism is useful, we need a controlled timing protocol.
Evidence
overlap_gate; overlap_stress; docs/prefix_publication.md

We found a numerical execution issue before publication. Single-token AR and batched
verification could use different arithmetic and attention reductions. Small score differences can
flip a greedy token, after which the entire history changes. We introduced a versioned shared

outputs match that shared AR reference. However, shared AR changed the numerical
implementation too and differed from original AR on seven of nine diagnosed prompts. We
therefore report these as separate correctness contracts. Token matching within the shared
contract is established on the listed tests; original-wording equivalence and general answer
quality are not established.

Q&A practice · 1/5
Answer directly, then give one measurement and one limitation.
1. What is our research contribution?
Safe incremental publication and a compacted unequal-depth outcome-cache prototype, plus
reproducible evidence of when they help or add overhead. SSD itself and batch-independent
arithmetic are prior ideas; their baseline speedups are not our new scheduling result.
2. Have we beaten tuned complete SSD?
No. The completed publication comparison is about 1% slower than complete K7. Partial
unequal-depth results are also slower. K10 publication beats complete K10 in the context
diagnostic, but not the stronger complete K7 comparator.
3. What does P=4 actually mean?
At least four continuation tokens must be safely complete before that path is eligible to
publish. It may deliver more. P is fixed in uniform publication and does not refer to a
neural-network layer or a number of user requests.
4. How do uniform and overlap handoff differ?
The earlier path finishes already-submitted work before exposure. Overlap copies a completed
prefix into independent storage and can deliver it while the submitted tail finishes. It still joins
outstanding accesses before KV or graph-buffer reuse.
5. What has not run in the greedy successor?
GPU ownership tests, stress coverage, full-model correctness gates and performance
measurements are queued. CPU validation exists, but it is insufficient to establish correctness
of the asynchronous GPU lifetime behavior or a latency improvement.

Q&A practice · 2/5
Answer directly, then give one measurement and one limitation.
6. What is F and what is unequal depth?
F is the recovery-candidate fan-out per possible acceptance position. F3 gives three candidate
ranks. Unequal depth assigns those ranks different continuation budgets, such as [7,4,4],
instead of preparing seven tokens for every rank.
7. Is the unequal-depth scheduler adaptive?
No. Its v1 budgets depend on static recovery rank and are identical across acceptance
positions. A separate local adaptive publication heuristic exists, but a learned/cost-aware
allocation policy from the proposal remains future work.
8. Why are fewer rows not automatically faster?
Small draft forwards can be memory- or launch-limited, so shrinking rows saves little time per
step. Shorter continuations may increase target verification rounds. Policy handling and
draining also cost time. Query-row reduction measures work, not full-request latency.
9. Why include reversed and equal-work controls?
Reversal tests whether prioritizing higher-ranked candidates matters. Uniform equal-work
depth tests whether the uneven allocation beats a simpler matching query-row budget. Those
controls do not match every kernel, padding or memory-traffic cost.
10. Why can target verification be memory-bound?
At small query width, streaming the large target weights dominates. Several query rows reuse
those weights without proportionally increasing elapsed time. Historical Nsight Compute
counters place verification left of the measured roofline ridge, with high memory utilization.

Q&A practice · 3/5
Answer directly, then give one measurement and one limitation.
11. What does Nsight Systems capture?
A correlated CPU/GPU timeline: host ranges, CUDA API submissions, kernels, streams, copies
and communication. Our NVTX labels connect these events to prefill, drafting, verification and
handoff. It reveals where time and dependencies occur.
12. What does Nsight Compute add?
Hardware-counter analysis of selected kernels or phases: arithmetic intensity, DRAM traffic,
computation/memory utilization, occupancy and stalls. Our five historical captures classify
decode, verify, draft, tree step and prefill. Profiling is kept separate from headline timing.
13. Does 92% GPU busy mean good utilization?
Not necessarily. In the historical target trace, about 35% of the full window is NCCL kernel
time, which can include waiting for peer data. Kernel activity is different from useful model
progress or arithmetic-unit utilization.
14. How much of 9.2 ms is actual transfer?
We have no isolated exact transfer share. About 8.0 ms aligns with unfinished-tree completion
in the historical trace, but the residual also contains host handling and synchronization. The
queued correlated timestamp diagnostic is designed to locate the remaining opportunity.
15. Does a separate CUDA stream prove overlap?
No. Dependencies and GPU resource constraints can serialize it. We record responses
completed before the submitted tail event finishes. Actual event coverage establishes overlap;
only full-request comparisons establish whether it improves latency.

Q&A practice · 4/5
Answer directly, then give one measurement and one limitation.
16. Why did original AR and speculation disagree?
Different execution shapes selected different finite-precision arithmetic/reduction paths. Close
target logits could change order and flip a greedy token. Matching-prefix projection and
attention controls supplied evidence. Later text diverges because the histories then differ.
17. What does the shared numerical reference fix?
It gives AR and speculative verification consistent target arithmetic for the tested scope.
Outputs match shared-fixed16-v1 AR. It does not guarantee original AR wording: shared AR
differed on seven of nine diagnosed prompts, and historical reproduction remained a separate
failed gate.
18. Are sixteen padded rows a batch of sixteen requests?
No. They are an internal shape used for consistent target operations. The request batch size
remains one. Verification rows, outcome-tree rows, continuation depth and simultaneous user
requests are distinct dimensions.
19. Why are AR and SD on one GPU but SSD on two?
That is the declared implementation allocation: AR target-only, SD both models on GPU0, SSD
target GPU0 and draft GPU1. The result is a latency comparison with additional SSD hardware,
not a claim of identical resource efficiency.
20. Why reuse AR rather than rerun every campaign?
Saved exact output references are hash-validated against the model, inputs, numerical
contract and dedicated receipts, with short fresh continuity checks. Historical timings stay
labeled separately. Fresh SSD-versus-SSD arms provide the primary policy comparison.

Q&A practice · 5/5
Answer directly, then give one measurement and one limitation.
21. What is the point of two output protocols?
Fixed256 controls output work by ignoring EOS. Natural EOS measures variable response
lengths up to the same cap. Both use winners selected on fixed-length tuning. Reporting
lengths and agreement prevents confusing shorter output with faster execution.
22. What does five repetitions establish?
It estimates repeated-run variation and output repeatability on the same prompts. It does not
create new tasks. Our hierarchical paired bootstrap preserves round/prompt grouping and
family balance rather than treating 375 measurements as unrelated prompts.
23. Did longer contexts help our method?
They exposed a useful K10 partial-handoff regime, but complete K7 remained faster at every
measured input length. The trend was not monotonic, and the synthetic three-template sweep
does not establish unseen long-document generalization.
24. Why not claim the CPU-tested successor works already?
The CPU tests cannot exercise actual cross-stream writes, consumer lifetime and full-model
output behavior. Its queued CUDA and generation gates must pass before performance, and
every optimized arm must be compared with unchanged legacy complete SSD.
25. What would change your conclusion next?
A fully validated version with lower full-request latency than tuned complete SSD, supported by
paired repeated results and disclosed family regressions. Timestamp evidence should explain
the mechanism; a stronger unseen workload should test whether the effect generalizes.
